Where it looks

First the face is found and followed, frame by frame.

Only three patches matter: the forehead and both cheeks, where thin skin covers dense small vessels.

Then the picture is thrown away. No face image or face geometry is stored. Only numbers.

How it works · rPPG

Every heartbeat pushes a little more blood into the tiny vessels under your skin.

Blood soaks up green light, so a little less light bounces back with each beat.

The camera records that change. It is far too small for your eyes to see.

Filtering turns it into a clean pulse wave. That is rPPG: your heartbeat, read from light.

An 80-year-old idea, now in your camera

Pulse oximeters have used light to read the blood since the 1940s.

1940s

The first oximeter

Glen Millikan builds a portable ear oximeter to protect pilots from oxygen loss at altitude during the Second World War.

1975

Pulse oximetry is born

Nihon Kohden's OLV-5100 ear oximeter uses the pulse in the light signal, the principle every pulse oximeter still uses.

1977

The finger clip

Minolta's OXIMET MET-1471 moves the sensor to the fingertip.

1980s

Into US hospitals

Biox brings pulse oximeters to the US market. Within a decade they are standard in recovery rooms and ICUs.

2000s

Beyond oxygen

Light-based sensors start to estimate hemoglobin without a needle.

2019

Blood pressure from light

A wearable PPG sensor reaches the market for cuffless blood pressure monitoring.

Today

No sensor at all

VitalKick reads the same pulse signal from video of your face. The sensor is the camera you already own.
